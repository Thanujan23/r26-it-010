    Final-year research project · R26-IT-010

    AI-Based Electricity Demand Intelligence System for Sri Lanka

    Planners receive demand figures and forecasts but little insight into what kind of day each day is, which days behave abnormally, and why. The system combines four services: behaviour intelligence, adaptive deep-learning forecasting with calibrated uncertainty, explainable AI and scenario decision support.

    Read the domain Contact the team

    Four components

    Each member builds one service. Together they tell a planner what demand will be, how the day behaves, why, and what happens if conditions change.

      Research domain

      Literature Survey

      Electricity cannot be stored cheaply at grid scale, so generation has to follow demand closely, and the quality of the information planners hold about demand has direct operational and financial consequences. Research on electricity demand has concentrated on forecasting: statistical models such as ARIMA and regression were followed by machine-learning and deep-learning models that predict demand more accurately. The survey below follows the four themes of our proposals.

      Demand forecasting in Sri Lanka

      A study of peak electricity demand in Sri Lanka compared multiple regression analysis with an artificial neural network on Ceylon Electricity Board data and reported a mean absolute percentage error of 2.50% for the neural network against 4.72% for regression [1]. A meta-analysis covering Australia, Pakistan and Sri Lanka found that machine-learning and hybrid methods generally outperform statistical models, but that many models do not adequately include external factors such as weather and behaviour [2]. Short-term studies for Sri Lanka have compared statistical and deep-learning models [3], and a broad review of forecasting methodologies identified interpretability and behavioural analysis as weaknesses of current models [4].

      Clustering of load patterns

      K-Means has been used to classify load patterns of the Indonesian Jawa-Bali system by their peak-load characteristics, revealing variation associated with national holidays and social activity [5]. Unsupervised methods with dynamic time warping have grouped industrial demand profiles for demand-side management [6], and Chicco reviewed clustering methods for load-pattern grouping together with the indices used to evaluate them [7]. Other work clusters households or smart-meter series first and then forecasts per cluster, which reduced forecast error [8], [9]. In these studies clustering is a preprocessing aid, not a description of daily system behaviour.

      Anomaly detection in electricity consumption

      A hybrid framework combining unsupervised detection with a supervised boosted decision tree was applied to smart-meter data from about 4,300 Irish households and reported 90% accuracy and an F1-score of 0.894 in identifying suspicious consumption [10]. A VAE–DBSCAN method removed abnormal readings before ARIMA forecasting [11], and a framework using symbolic aggregate approximation with CART and MLP classifiers gave energy managers interpretable anomaly tools for buildings [12]. These frameworks work at meter or building level, and several depend on labelled anomalies.

      Choosing unsupervised models without ground truth

      Without labels, cluster quality is judged with internal indices: the Silhouette coefficient [13], the Davies–Bouldin index [14] and the Calinski–Harabasz score [15]. The indices encode different notions of a good partition and can disagree. For anomaly detectors, the number of flagged points is fixed in advance by the contamination parameter, so counting anomalies says little about quality; Hennig's bootstrap approach, which compares results across resamples with the Jaccard coefficient, offers a stability-based alternative [16].

      Research Gap

      Existing work predicts demand well, but each capability a planner needs sits in a separate study or tool. The proposals identified five gaps.

      1. No day-level behaviour description

        Clustering of load profiles is mostly used to improve forecasts or to profile customers, and anomaly detection targets fraud and faults in individual meters. No reviewed study or tool assigns each day of a system demand series to a small set of readable behaviour categories with reasons.

      2. Point forecasts without uncertainty

        Most forecasting research reports accuracy measures such as MAE and RMSE. These say nothing about how uncertain a forecast is, and hybrid and attention-based architectures are rarely compared side by side.

      3. Forecasts that cannot be explained

        Many models operate as black boxes and return point estimates without showing how weather, calendar or other factors drove them, which limits trust and use by planners and policymakers.

      4. No what-if scenario analysis

        Most solutions lack interactive scenario analysis, so decision-makers cannot easily simulate conditions such as heat waves, festivals or demand spikes before they occur.

      5. No unified platform

        Research rarely combines behaviour analysis, forecasting, explanation and decision support in one practical tool; the intelligence a planner needs is fragmented.

      A further, methodological gap concerns how unsupervised models are chosen. Comparisons often rely on a single index such as the Silhouette score, which can favour degenerate solutions, and anomaly detectors are often compared by how many points they flag, which is set in advance by their contamination parameter.

      Research Problem

      The decision problem is bigger than a single demand prediction. How can 15-minute electricity demand data be turned into intelligence that tells a planner what demand will be, what kind of day it is, why, and what would happen under changed conditions — in one connected system?

      Table 2 — Four parts of the problem
      No.ProblemWhy it matters
      01Point forecasts hide operating riskA single value does not show the plausible best- and worst-case demand range.
      02Static models can ageLoad relationships shift with weather, calendars, events and changing demand patterns.
      03Numbers alone lack behavioural contextOperators still need to know whether a day is normal, peak, low or genuinely abnormal.
      04Separate tools weaken decision flowForecasting, anomaly review, explanation and planning should connect through one platform.

      A related difficulty is that clustering and anomaly detection are unsupervised. Without labelled abnormal days it is not obvious how to choose between algorithms or how to show that a choice is justified, and published systems seldom explain their choice.

      Research Objectives

      Main objective

      To design, implement and evaluate a decision-support system that combines behavioural analysis of demand, short-term forecasting with uncertainty, explainable AI and scenario-based decision support for electricity planners in Sri Lanka, delivered as connected services behind one dashboard.

      Specific objectives

      1. Behaviour intelligence

        What type of demand day is this — and is it abnormal?

        • Preprocess the 15-minute demand data and build 96-point daily demand profiles.
        • Apply and compare clustering algorithms to group days with similar profiles, selecting a model with a justified multi-index procedure.
        • Apply and compare anomaly-detection methods to flag abnormal days, using a procedure that does not depend on the preset anomaly rate.
        • Generate interpretable behaviour labels — Normal Weekday, Peak Demand, Holiday / Low Demand and Abnormal Demand Day — with risk levels and written reasons.
      2. Adaptive forecasting and prediction

        What will demand be — and how uncertain is it?

        • Develop and compare deep-learning models, including CNN, LSTM, Transformer and hybrid architectures.
        • Produce probabilistic forecasts with Monte Carlo Dropout and report a demand range, not only a point value.
        • Assess forecast uncertainty through the coverage of the prediction intervals.
      3. Explainable AI

        Which features drove the prediction?

        • Add an explainable-AI component using SHAP and LIME that gives global and local explanations of variable importance.
        • Present feature contributions visually and in text so that planners can understand and trust the output.
      4. Scenario and decision support

        What happens under a changed condition?

        • Build a scenario engine that lets planners test what-if situations against a baseline.
        • Assess the risk of each scenario and provide actionable suggestions through an easy-to-use dashboard.
        • Test and validate the module for accuracy, responsiveness and usability.

      Methodology

      The project follows Design Science Research, in which an artefact is built and evaluated to solve an identified problem [17], [18]: identify the problem, define objectives, design and develop, demonstrate, evaluate and communicate. The design–evaluation loop was repeated after each progress review.

      Table 3 — Method by component
      ComponentPipelineEvaluation
      Behaviour Intelligence Clean the series and pivot it into 1,977 daily profiles of 96 slots; cluster profile shapes (K-Means, Ward, Gaussian Mixture, DBSCAN); detect abnormal days (Isolation Forest, LOF, One-Class SVM, Z-score); find peak days with percentile rules; combine the outputs into four labels by priority. Mean rank over Silhouette, Davies–Bouldin and Calinski–Harabasz for clustering; bootstrap Jaccard stability for detectors; temporal hold-out for future-behaviour prediction.
      Adaptive Forecasting 20 input features from demand, weather, calendar, events and lagged load; 96-step lookback, one-step horizon, train-only scaling; CNN, LSTM, Transformer and two hybrid models; 100 stochastic Monte Carlo Dropout passes with residual-based calibration. MAE and model comparison; empirical coverage of the 95% interval.
      Explainable AI LIME and SHAP applied to the forecasting models to rank feature contributions for a single prediction (local) and across the data (global); explanations of abnormal days opened from the behaviour dashboard. Reliability of explanations and of feature-importance rankings; how well users understand the visualisations.
      Scenario & Decision Support Baseline input (demand, weather, calendar, events); apply a scenario such as a heat wave, cold spell, price shock or economic boom; predict with a Random Forest / stacking-ensemble model; compare with the baseline as a percentage change in demand. Model comparison; accuracy, responsiveness and usability of the module.
